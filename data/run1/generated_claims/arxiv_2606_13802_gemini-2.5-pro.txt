Based on the LaTeX source provided, here are the major, unique, and falsifiable claims made in the paper:

1.  A majority (68%) of user actions in the curated spreadsheet creation trajectories are, in principle, predictable from the preceding edit history and visual state.
2.  Fine-tuning a relatively small language model (360M parameters) on synthetic spreadsheet operation sequences can achieve next-action prediction performance (26.8% user actions saved) comparable to that of a much larger, general-purpose zero-shot model like GPT-5 (27.4% user actions saved).
3.  An online evaluation framework that dynamically updates the ground-truth based on accepted predictions provides a more realistic measure of a system's utility than a static, offline (teacher-forced) evaluation, as it accounts for compounding errors and the system's ability to revise its own suggestions.
4.  Without an acceptance heuristic to filter out poor suggestions, predictive systems result in a net negative impact on user productivity (a 19.2% increase in user actions required).
5.  Invoking a prediction system more frequently (e.g., after every user action) leads to a greater overall reduction in user effort than less frequent invocations, even if the per-prediction acceptance rate is lower.
6.  Acceptance heuristics that prioritize utility (i.e., net actions saved) yield higher overall user action savings than more conservative heuristics that also require high or perfect precision.
7.  The performance of next-action prediction systems improves as more context becomes available; prediction acceptance rates are significantly higher in the later stages of a spreadsheet's creation than in the initial stages.
8.  Content-generating spreadsheet operations (e.g., `input`, `paste`, `fill`) are systematically more predictable and result in higher acceptance rates across different models than presentational formatting operations (e.g., `align`, `border`).
9.  Increasing the context window of past user actions improves prediction performance, but this effect has diminishing returns, with performance plateauing at a context length of approximately 128 operations.
10. In multi-action prediction settings, allowing a model to determine its own prediction length results in higher user action savings than externally capping the number of predicted actions.
11. Within a single multi-action prediction, the accuracy of the predicted actions decays as a function of their position in the sequence; the first actions in a prediction block are more likely to be correct than later ones.
12. The reduction in user effort from predictive systems is not uniform but occurs in "bursts," where a majority of the savings comes from short sequences of consecutively accepted predictions ("acceptance streaks").
