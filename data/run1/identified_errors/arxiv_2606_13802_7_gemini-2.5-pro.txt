\paragraph{Precision alone does not ensure utility.}

Even at \textsc{p90}, which accepts any prediction with $\geq 90\%$ precision regardless of utility, the system delivers only 17.0\% \textsc{uas} -- 4.8 points below \textsc{hybrid-1} (21.8\%) and 5.3 points below \textsc{greedy} (22.3\%) (Table~\ref{tab:heuristics-results}).
\textsc{p100} (perfect precision) does better at 19.9\% but is still surpassed by \textsc{hybrid-1}.
Precision measures correctness of \emph{individual} predicted properties, not whether a prediction actually advances the user toward completion; a heuristic that ignores utility leaves savings on the table.

:explanation:
The experimental results in Table 5 contain contradictions that undermine the validity of the evaluation framework. The primary and most serious flaw is in the reported Acceptance Rates (AR).

1.  The `greedy` heuristic, which accepts any prediction that saves at least one user action ($\ell \ge 1$), is reported to have an acceptance rate of 20.0%.
2.  The `p100` heuristic, which only accepts predictions with perfect precision ($p=1$), is reported to have a higher acceptance rate of 21.8%.

This result is highly counter-intuitive and suggests a fundamental error. The `greedy` heuristic should accept a broader class of predictions than `p100`, as it can accept imperfect but still useful suggestions (e.g., a prediction with high true positives and few false positives). In contrast, `p100` is far stricter. A higher acceptance rate for `p100` implies a flaw in the implementation of the heuristics or the "user actions saved" (\textsc{uas}) metric, which would cast doubt on all reported results. The accompanying text also fails to acknowledge or explain this anomaly.

A secondary issue in the same table is the misleading presentation of the average precision (`Prec`). For the `p100` heuristic, the table reports an average precision of 38.2%. While this is mathematically possible if `Prec` is a macro-average over all predictions (both accepted and rejected), it is deeply confusing to report such a low precision value for a heuristic that, by definition, only accepts perfectly precise predictions. This flawed reporting makes the results difficult to interpret and trust.
