**Self-Generated vs. External Reflection.**
Using reflections generated by a larger external teacher (Qwen3-32B) performs worse than self-reflection (-1.8 points on AIME'24).
This is because an external reflection re-introduces the fundamental challenge of distillation: it forces the student to align with a policy conditioned on information that is outside its own representational capacity. By providing guidance generated by a stronger model, we are asking the student to model a reasoning process it cannot fully represent, which leads to a degradation in performance compared to the purely self-generated signal.

:explanation:
The paper's central claim is that its success stems from using self-reflection, which avoids a "severe distribution shift" that occurs when using reflections from an external teacher. The paper argues that an external reflection creates a target distribution that the student model "cannot represent," leading to a "degradation in performance."

However, the paper's own ablation study in Table 4 contradicts this explanation. While using an external teacher for reflections (71.5% on AIME'24) is slightly worse than using self-reflection (73.3%), it is a massive improvement over the no-reflection baseline (65.8%). The external reflection accounts for a large majority of the total performance gain.

Characterizing this highly effective technique as a "degradation" caused by an "unrepresentable" distribution is a severe misinterpretation of the results. The data clearly shows the student model *can* effectively leverage the external reflection. Therefore, the core explanatory mechanism proposed for the method's success is not supported by the paper's own evidence, creating a major internal contradiction.
