The paper's central premise is that performance in long-context decoding is limited by memory bandwidth, and that reducing I/O by scanning a 2-bit quantized representation of the K-cache should therefore improve speed.

However, the component-wise ablation study presented in Appendix B contains a result that directly contradicts this premise and is justified with flawed reasoning. The study reports that switching from a full-precision (FP16) scan to a 2-bit scan for a standard top-k selection strategy *decreases* end-to-end throughput from 17.43 tok/s to 14.80 tok/s. This result is highly counter-intuitive, as an 8x reduction in data loaded from HBM during the costly scanning phase should lead to a significant speedup.

The paper's explanation for this phenomenon, "due to compute-bound sorting overhead," is illogical. The computational cost of sorting the attention scores is independent of the precision used to generate them; the number of scores to sort remains the same. A faster scan cannot increase the absolute time of a subsequent sorting step. This critical flaw in the ablation study undermines the paper's argument for the synergistic necessity of its proposed components, suggesting a fundamental error in the experiments or their interpretation.
