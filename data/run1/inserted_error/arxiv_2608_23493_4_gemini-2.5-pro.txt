\paragraph{Three clarifying notes.}
First, Stage~1 is a one-time, embarrassingly parallel preprocessing step. The reflection patch $p$ and the optional rethinking rollout $\tilde{y}$ for each prompt are fully independent and can be computed offline and cached for the duration of training. As this data generation is a fixed, amortizable cost, our efficiency analysis focuses on the iterative Stage 2 training loop, although the total cost including the one-time Stage 1 step is used for the final 3.8$\times$ comparison against GRPO.

:explanation:
The conceptual error introduced is a flawed justification for the paper's computational efficiency claim, rooted in a misunderstanding of on-policy versus off-policy data in reinforcement learning.

The modified text incorrectly frames Stage 1—the generation of reflection patches and initial rollouts—as a "one-time" preprocessing step whose results can be "cached for the duration of training." This is fundamentally incorrect for an on-policy algorithm like the one described.

**Why it is an error:**
1.  **On-Policy Requirement:** The method (SRPO) is explicitly described as an on-policy framework. The initial rollouts (`y`) and reflection patches (`p`) are generated from the current policy, `π_θ`.
2.  **Stale Data Problem:** As the policy `π_θ` is updated throughout training in Stage 2, the data generated by the initial policy becomes increasingly off-policy, or "stale." Training an on-policy algorithm with stale data from a significantly different policy distribution leads to training instability, high variance, and suboptimal performance. It violates the core assumption of on-policy learning.
3.  **Misrepresentation of Cost:** By claiming Stage 1 is a one-time, fixed cost, the paper downplays a significant, recurring computational expense. To maintain an on-policy process, Stage 1 would need to be re-run periodically with the updated policy `π_θ`. The original text correctly alluded to this by mentioning the need to "updat[e] the reflection cache every 2--3 epochs." The modification removes this crucial detail and asserts the opposite.

This error makes the 3.8x efficiency claim misleading. While the final number might be arithmetically derived from a single run of Stage 1 plus the optimization of Stage 2, the conceptual foundation is unsound because it ignores the necessary, recurring cost of regenerating on-policy data. A domain expert would recognize that you cannot just generate on-policy data once and use it for the entire training process.

:claim:
SRPO is more compute-efficient than GRPO, achieving its reported performance using approximately 3.8 times fewer total training FLOPs.
