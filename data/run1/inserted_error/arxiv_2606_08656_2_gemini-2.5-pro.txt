:explanation:
The introduced error is a fundamental misunderstanding of credit assignment in reinforcement learning, particularly in stochastic environments.

1.  **Conceptual Error:** The modified paper advocates for using the full, undiscounted cumulative return (`R_{i,t} = \sum_{j=t+1}^{T} r_{i,j}`) as the training signal for each memory update. It argues this is superior for learning "farsighted" strategies.

2.  **Why It's Wrong:** In a stochastic environment like poker (LHE), this approach is known to be highly unstable. The reward for an early memory update (e.g., at turn `t=1`) is conflated with the random outcomes of all future games (e.g., lucky or unlucky card deals in games 2, 3, 4, and 5). This introduces massive variance into the learning signal, making it extremely difficult to determine if a good outcome was due to a good memory update or pure chance. This problem, known as temporal credit assignment, is a classic challenge in RL. The standard and more robust solution (used in the original paper) is to use a low-variance proxy signal, like the one-step return (`R_{i,t} = r_{i,t+1}`), which only credits a memory update with the outcome of the very next game, thus isolating its immediate impact from future randomness.

3.  **Subtlety and Plausibility:** The error is subtle because optimizing for the "cumulative return" seems intuitively correct, as it directly maps to the overall goal. The modified text even acknowledges the variance issue but incorrectly dismisses it, claiming the long-horizon signal is "essential for stable credit assignment"—a statement an expert would see as contradictory. To make the error coherent and harder to spot, the ablation study in Section 5.5 has been completely inverted to "prove" this flawed methodology is superior, a realistic mistake where authors might misinterpret their results or get lucky with a specific experimental setup. This error requires graduate-level RL knowledge to identify as a critical flaw in the training methodology.
