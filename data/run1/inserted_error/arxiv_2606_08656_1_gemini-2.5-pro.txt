:explanation:
The introduced error is a subtle but critical misinterpretation of the model's learned capabilities, stemming from a core design choice in the reinforcement learning setup.

1.  **The Design Choice:** The paper uses a one-step (next-game) reward signal ($r_{t+1}$) to train the memory update policy. It correctly justifies this as a variance reduction technique for training stability.
2.  **The Unacknowledged Implication:** In reinforcement learning, using a myopic (one-step) reward biases the policy to learn actions that yield immediate rewards, even at the expense of long-term cumulative performance. The introduced modifications frame this as a positive ("learning immediate, exploitative responses").
3.  **The Conceptual Error:** The paper then observes that the memory model transfers successfully to a stronger player and incorrectly concludes that it has learned a "robust memory update behavior that extracts transferable strategic signals". The more likely, but unstated, explanation is that the myopic reward signal trained the memory model to generate simple, reactive, short-horizon heuristics. The stronger player (Qwen3-235B) performs better not because the learned *strategy* is deeply generalizable, but because stronger LLMs are simply better at executing any kind of clear, concise, short-term instruction.

The error is that the authors misattribute the cause of their successful generalization. They believe they have taught the model deep, transferable strategic reasoning, when in fact they have only taught it to generate simple, reactive plans. This is a plausible oversight, as the model's empirical success masks the shallow nature of what was actually learned, a common pitfall in RL-based agent training. An expert would recognize that the myopic reward function is at odds with the claim of learning complex, long-horizon strategies.
