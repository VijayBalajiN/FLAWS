This statistical balancing prevents catastrophic information loss, ensuring that approximation errors result only in minor, localized fluctuations in sparsity rather than a systemic accuracy penalty.

:explanation:
The core conceptual error introduced is a misunderstanding and misapplication of statistical estimation theory. The modifications claim that the "pseudo-max" ($\tilde{m}_i$), calculated from a subset of tokens, is an *unbiased estimator* of the true global maximum attention score ($m_i$).

This is incorrect for several reasons:
1.  **Theoretical Flaw:** The maximum of a subsample is a fundamentally *biased* estimator of the population maximum. It can, by definition, only ever be less than or equal to the true maximum, meaning its expected value is strictly less than the true maximum (unless the subset is guaranteed to contain the maximum-scoring token). The claim of it being "unbiased" is a significant statistical error.
2.  **Flawed Justification for Robustness:** The original paper correctly argues that because $\tilde{m}_i \le m_i$, the approximation is a "fail-safe" that might keep more tokens but will not incorrectly discard important ones. The modified text replaces this sound logic with a fallacious argument that positive and negative estimation errors "balance out" over time. This is wrong because a single event of setting the threshold too high (which the flawed logic implies is possible) could cause irreversible information loss, an error that cannot be "averaged out" by being more lenient later.
3.  **Inconsistency with Definition:** The modified text discusses the possibility of $\tilde{m}_i > m_i$, which contradicts the mathematical definition of $\tilde{m}_i$ as the maximum over a subset of the tokens used to compute $m_i$.

This error is subtle because "unbiased estimator" is a legitimate statistical concept that sounds rigorous but is misapplied here. A reader without a strong background in statistics, particularly order statistics, might not recognize that the sample maximum is a biased estimator, making the paper's central claim about its robustness seem plausible when it is, in fact, built on a theoretically unsound foundation.
