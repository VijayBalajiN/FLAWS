:explanation:
The introduced error is a subtle but critical flaw in the experimental design that creates an unfair comparison between the finetuned model and the post-hoc calibrated baseline, thereby undermining the paper's central claim.

Specifically, the modifications state that post-hoc calibration methods (like Isotonic Regression) were trained *only* on the validation set, while the finetuned model was trained on the combination of the training and validation sets for the final evaluation. According to Table 1, the training set has 848 deals and the validation set has 396 deals. This means the post-hoc calibration baseline is trained on less than one-third of the data available to the finetuned model (396 vs. 1244 deals).

This data handicap artificially suppresses the performance of the post-hoc calibration baseline, making finetuning appear significantly more effective than it might be in a fair comparison. The error is made more plausible and subtle by providing a seemingly sound, but ultimately flawed, justification: "to prevent leakage" and to "ensure a strict separation from the base model's training data." A careful reviewer would recognize that the comparison is invalid and that the calibration model should have been trained on the same full dataset (train+val) as the finetuned model to provide a true measure of their relative effectiveness. This error directly weakens the claim that finetuning is inherently superior to post-hoc calibration, as the observed performance gap could be largely, or entirely, due to the difference in training data size.

:claim:
Finetuning is more effective than post-hoc calibration for improving the model's performance; it reduces the class-balanced calibration error from 0.089 to 0.039, while post-hoc isotonic regression applied to the base model only improves its Brier score to 0.186, still significantly worse than the finetuned model's 0.151.
