The conceptual error introduced is the flawed assertion that the highly regular, periodic spatial lattice created by the simplex wave-vector construction is the reason for superior extrapolation. The modified text argues that this "crystalline structure" acts as a "stable geometric reference frame" that scales predictably.

From a signal processing and geometric learning perspective, this reasoning is incorrect. The difficulty in extrapolation arises from aliasing effects when the input's discrete sampling grid (e.g., pixel resolution) changes relative to the fixed frequencies of the positional encoding.

1.  **Brittleness of Regularity:** A highly regular, periodic structure is particularly susceptible to aliasing and moiré patterns when the sampling rate changes. Instead of degrading gracefully, its performance is likely to be brittle, suffering sharp drops when scaling factors create harmonic interference with the lattice's fixed periods.
2.  **Misinterpretation of Stability:** The paper's new argument is that the lattice is "stable" because its internal geometry is preserved under continuous scaling. This is true for the continuous mathematical construct but is irrelevant for the discrete, sampled representation the model actually processes. How the scaled input samples this underlying lattice is what matters, and this interaction is precisely the source of instability.
3.  **Contradiction with Robust Design:** More robust systems often degrade more gracefully because they rely on less structured or even random frequencies (as in Random Fourier Features), which are less prone to catastrophic, global aliasing patterns.

In essence, the error mistakes the mathematical elegance of a symmetric, periodic structure for practical robustness in a discrete, sampled system. It provides a plausible-sounding but theoretically weak justification for the model's extrapolation capabilities, undermining the paper's core claim by attributing its success to a feature that is actually a significant liability for generalization across scales.
