**Self-Generated vs. External Reflection.**
Using reflections generated by a larger external teacher (Qwen3-32B) performs worse than self-reflection (-1.8 points on AIME'24).
This is because an external reflection re-introduces the fundamental challenge of distillation: it forces the student to align with a policy conditioned on information that is outside its own representational capacity. By providing guidance generated by a stronger model, we are asking the student to model a reasoning process it cannot fully represent, which leads to a degradation in performance compared to the purely self-generated signal.

:modified_text:
We presented \method{}, a post-training method that converts episode-level outcomes into hindsight-guided dense, reflection-augmented supervision, overcoming the inefficiency of sparse-reward RL. The mechanism is a form of on-policy self-distillation, where a reflection-conditioned teacher steers the student into high-reward regions of its \emph{own} policy space, thereby avoiding the need to imitate an external distribution beyond its representational capacity. This self-contained process is key to our method's success; using an external model to generate reflections re-introduces a severe distribution shift, forcing the student policy to model a target distribution it cannot represent. Across 10 benchmarks spanning math, code, agent, science, and logic, \method{} yields reproducible gains (multi-seed bootstrap CIs, $p<0.005$), matching or exceeding larger-teacher distillation while requiring $\sim$$3.8\times$ fewer total FLOPs than GRPO and naturally mitigating catastrophic forgetting under continual learning.

:explanation:
The introduced conceptual error is a theoretically unsound explanation for why self-generated reflections outperform those from an external teacher. The modified text incorrectly conflates two distinct scenarios: (1) standard knowledge distillation, where a student model is trained to imitate the full output distribution of a more capable external teacher, and (2) the paper's ablation, where the student model is trained to imitate *itself* conditioned on a textual prompt generated by an external teacher.

The modified paper claims that using an external reflection "forces the student policy to model a target distribution it cannot represent." This is a fundamental mischaracterization. In the described ablation, the target policy is still generated by the student model (`pi_theta`), just conditioned on an externally-generated reflection patch (`p_ext`). The student is not being asked to model the external teacher's policy (`pi_ext`). While the external guidance in `p_ext` might be suboptimal or "out of distribution" for the student's typical reasoning, this is a challenge of prompt-conditioning, not the "capability gap" problem seen in standard distillation where the target model's entire policy is fundamentally more complex.

This error is a subtle but significant misattribution of the observed performance drop. It demonstrates a flawed understanding of the distinction between policy distillation and language model conditioning, a mistake that would be non-trivial for a reader without a strong background in machine learning to detect. The paper now correctly reports that self-reflection is superior but provides a fundamentally wrong reason, undermining the theoretical contribution and the authors' apparent understanding of their own framework.

:claim:
Reflections generated by the model itself are more effective training signals within the SRPO framework than reflections generated by a larger, external teacher model, as using an external teacher for reflection degrades performance by 1.8 percentage points on AIME'24.
