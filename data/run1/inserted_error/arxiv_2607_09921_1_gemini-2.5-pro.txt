:original_text:
We model the downside $S^-_t$, the expected target price if the deal fails, by allowing standalone value to evolve with market movements scaled by the target’s pre-announcement beta $\beta$:
$$
S^-_t = S^-_0 \exp\bigl(\beta \, r_{m,[0,t]}\bigr),
$$
where $S^-_0$ is the 20-day pre-announcement average price and $r_{m,[0,t]}$ is the cumulative log return of the market index from announcement to day $t$. While this specification captures the effect of prevailing market conditions, realized post-break prices also depend on the reason for termination and the potential for alternative transactions, and therefore vary substantially in practice.

:modified_text:
We model the downside $S^-_t$, the expected target price if the deal fails, as the target’s pre-announcement standalone price. This value, $S^-_0$, is calculated as the 20-day average share price prior to the deal announcement. We assume this downside price remains constant throughout the deal’s lifecycle, as it represents the intrinsic value of the company absent the transaction:
$$
S^-_t = S^-_0.
$$
While this specification provides a clear and stable baseline, realized post-break prices also depend on the reason for termination and the potential for alternative transactions, and therefore vary substantially in practice.

:explanation:
The conceptual error introduced is the simplification of the downside risk calculation for the market-implied probability ($p^m_t$). The original, correct formula modeled the downside price ($S^-_t$) as a dynamic value that evolves with market movements, adjusted by the company's beta. The modification replaces this with a static downside, fixed at the pre-announcement share price ($S^-_0$).

This is a significant theoretical oversight in financial modeling for the following reasons:
1.  **Ignores Market Drift:** The standalone value of a company is not static. Over the lifecycle of a merger (which can take many months), the broader stock market can move significantly. A static downside assumes the company's value, should the deal fail, is independent of these market-wide changes. This is unrealistic.
2.  **Creates a Flawed Benchmark:** The market-implied probability is a crucial benchmark for evaluating the models. By using a static downside, the calculated $p^m_t$ becomes an inaccurate measure of the market's true consensus, especially for long-duration deals or in volatile markets.
3.  **Undermines the Key Claim:** The paper claims the LLM's low correlation with $p^m_t$ (0.358) means it provides novel information. However, this introduced error provides an alternative explanation: the LLM's forecasts may be correctly incorporating market drift, causing them to diverge from the *incorrectly calculated* static-downside $p^m_t$. Conversely, the XGBoost model's high correlation (0.753) is likely an artifact of being trained directly on this flawed feature, rather than a sign of it being "tied to market consensus." The entire comparison of model informativeness is thus built on a methodologically weak foundation.

This error is subtle because the justification for a "stable baseline" sounds plausible to a non-expert, but it represents a common yet critical mistake in event-driven quantitative finance.

:claim:
The forecasts generated by the finetuned system are less correlated with market-implied probabilities (Pearson $\rho=0.358$) than those from the XGBoost baseline ($\rho=0.753$), suggesting the model provides information not fully captured by market consensus.
