:explanation:
The introduced error is a fundamental misunderstanding of the bias-variance tradeoff in reinforcement learning credit assignment, particularly in stochastic, multi-turn environments like poker (LHE).

1.  **The Error:** The paper has been modified to advocate for and use a long-horizon, undiscounted cumulative return for its advantage calculation (`R_{i,t} = \sum_{t'=t}^{T-1} r_{i,t'+1}`). The text now incorrectly argues that this is superior to a one-step proxy reward (`r_{t+1}`) because it captures the "true long-horizon goal." An ablation study in Section 5.5 has been falsified to provide "evidence" for this flawed claim.

2.  **Why It's Wrong:** While cumulative returns are unbiased estimators of the true value, they suffer from extremely high variance, especially in long episodes with significant environmental stochasticity (like different card deals in poker). This high variance makes the learning signal incredibly noisy, leading to unstable training and poor sample efficiency. In practice, this would make it very difficult for the model to learn a coherent strategy. The original paper correctly identified this pitfall and used a one-step reward as a lower-variance (though more biased) proxy to stabilize training.

3.  **Subtlety and Contradiction:** The error is subtle because the justification for using cumulative returns—to capture long-term consequences—is conceptually appealing and sounds correct to a non-expert. The core flaw undermines the paper's key claim that longer-horizon training rollouts (e.g., 5 games) lead to *more stable* learning. In reality, combining long rollouts with a high-variance cumulative reward signal would lead to *less* stability, as the variance of the return estimate compounds with each additional step. An expert LLM must connect the flawed reward mechanism described in Section 3.2 with the claim of stability in Section 5.3 and recognize the inherent theoretical tension between the two.

:claim:
Training the memory-update model with longer-horizon rollouts (e.g., 5-game episodes) leads to more stable and higher-performing test-time learning over extended interactions compared to training with shorter-horizon rollouts (e.g., 2-game episodes).
