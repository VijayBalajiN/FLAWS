:explanation:
The conceptual error introduced is a theoretically unsound formulation of the advantage function for the step-level affective reward. The modified text explicitly defines the local advantage, $A^S(t_i)$, as being equal to the immediate affective reward, $R_a(t_i)$.

From a reinforcement learning perspective, this is a significant flaw. The advantage function, $A(s, a)$, is meant to quantify how much better a particular action is compared to the average policy's performance from that state, considering the entire discounted future return. By equating the advantage with the immediate reward, the formulation becomes purely myopic. It encourages the agent to maximize the affective reward at the current step without any consideration for the long-term consequences of its actions on either future affective interactions or the overall task efficiency ($R_e$).

This myopic objective would likely lead to "reward hacking." For example, an agent might learn to send frequent but low-quality messages to maximize the communication reward component ($R_{com}$) of the affective reward, even if doing so wastes time and harms the final task outcome. This contradicts the paper's central claim that the training paradigm successfully balances and improves both efficiency and affective quality, making the impressive results reported in the paper theoretically suspect. An expert in RL would recognize that this approach to advantage estimation is biased and would fail to perform proper credit assignment for complex, long-horizon collaborative tasks.
