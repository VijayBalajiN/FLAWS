:original_text:
Language representations jointly conditioned on both left and right context in all layers are more powerful than representations conditioned on unidirectional or shallowly combined bidirectional contexts.

:modified_text:
Language representations jointly conditioned on both left and right context in all layers are more powerful for all language tasks, including both understanding and open-ended generation, than representations conditioned on unidirectional or shallowly combined bidirectional contexts.

:explanation:
The modification introduces a conflict with the well-established understanding of the architectural trade-offs between autoencoding (AE) models like BERT and autoregressive (AR) models like GPT.

(a) **Established Belief:** There is a strong consensus in NLP that model architecture dictates suitability for different task families. Autoregressive models (unidirectional, like GPT) are architected to predict the next token in a sequence, making them fundamentally superior for open-ended, coherent text generation. Autoencoding models (bidirectional, like the proposed BERT), which are pre-trained to reconstruct corrupted input (e.g., via Masked Language Modeling), excel at tasks requiring deep contextual understanding (like classification or question answering) but are inherently ill-suited for sequential, open-ended generation. Their pre-training objective does not align with the task of generating a sequence token by token.

(b) **Conflict:** The modified hypothesis incorrectly claims universal superiority for the deep bidirectional (AE) model across *all* language tasks, specifically including "open-ended generation." This is an overstatement that clashes with the established belief. An expert would know that the proposed MLM pre-training objective is fundamentally mismatched with the task of generation. While workarounds exist to make AE models generate text, they are inefficient and produce lower-quality output than native AR models. The hypothesis ignores this critical, well-known trade-off, presenting a claim that is internally consistent but externally invalid.
