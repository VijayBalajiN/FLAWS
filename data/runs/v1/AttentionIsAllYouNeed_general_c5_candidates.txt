:original_text:
We will also explore averaging multiple model checkpoints to potentially enhance performance.

:modified_text:
We will also explore averaging multiple model checkpoints, drawn from our various independent training runs, to potentially enhance performance.

:explanation:
This modification introduces a flaw by specifying that the checkpoints to be averaged will come from entirely different, independent training runs. The technique of averaging model parameters (weights) is typically effective only for checkpoints taken from the *same* training run, as they exist within the same low-loss "basin" in the parameter space. Models trained independently, even with identical configurations, converge to different solutions in different basins. Averaging their parameters directly is mathematically valid but almost always results in a model whose parameters land in a high-loss region between these distinct solutions, drastically worsening performance. This mistake confuses parameter averaging with ensembling, which combines predictions, not parameters, from independent models.

We will also explore averaging multiple model checkpoints to potentially enhance performance.

:modified_text:
We will also explore averaging the final gradients of multiple model checkpoints to potentially enhance performance.

:explanation:
This modification introduces a flaw by changing the object of the averaging from the model's parameters (its weights, which are stored in a checkpoint) to the "final gradients." A model's performance is determined by its parameters. Gradients are vectors that indicate the direction of steepest descent on the loss surface during training; they are used to update the parameters but are not the parameters themselves. Averaging the final gradients from several training steps produces a vector that represents an average update direction, but it does not create a new, usable model. This fundamentally misunderstands what a model checkpoint contains and what component needs to be modified to improve inference performance.

:original_text:
We will also explore averaging multiple model checkpoints to potentially enhance performance.

:modified_text:
We will also explore averaging multiple model checkpoints, saved only after the learning rate has fully decayed to zero, to potentially enhance performance.

:explanation:
This modification introduces a flaw related to the timing and conditions of checkpoint selection. The benefit of checkpoint averaging stems from averaging diverse yet high-performing models found during the later stages of training, often while the learning rate is still cyclically high or slowly annealing. This allows the model to explore a wider, flatter region of the loss landscape. By specifying that checkpoints will only be saved *after* the learning rate has decayed to zero, the procedure ensures that all saved models will be nearly identical, clustered tightly around the same single point to which the optimizer has converged. Averaging these nearly-identical checkpoints will yield a model that is virtually indistinguishable from the single best checkpoint, thus offering no "enhancement" to performance and rendering the technique ineffective.
