:error_type:
Conflict with Established Beliefs

:original_text:
The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its connection to the paper's core claims.

:modified_text:
The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its direct, localized connection to one of the paper's core claims, a relationship that can be assessed independently of the paper's auxiliary methodological assumptions.

:explanation:
The modification introduces a conflict with the Duhem-Quine thesis, a foundational concept in the philosophy of science.

(a) The established belief, the Duhem-Quine thesis, posits that it is impossible to test a scientific hypothesis in isolation. Any empirical test of a core claim (hypothesis) simultaneously tests the auxiliary assumptions and hypotheses that are required for the test to be valid (e.g., assumptions about the correctness of measurement instruments, the statistical model used, or background theory). Therefore, when an observation contradicts a prediction, one cannot be certain whether the core claim is false or if one of the many auxiliary assumptions is false.

(b) The modified hypothesis makes the explicit assertion that the criticality of an error—its relationship to a core claim—"can be assessed independently of the paper's auxiliary methodological assumptions." This is a direct contradiction of the Duhem-Quine thesis. While internally consistent, this claim is philosophically naive. It suggests a simplistic, local model of falsification where an error's impact can be neatly cordoned off to a single claim, ignoring the web of dependencies (the auxiliary assumptions) that give the claim its meaning and testability. An expert in epistemology or philosophy of science would recognize that this assertion of independence is untenable, as the "error" might only be an error in the context of those very assumptions.
