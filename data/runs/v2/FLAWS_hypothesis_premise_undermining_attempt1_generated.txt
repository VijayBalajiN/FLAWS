:error_type:
Undermining a Premise

:original_text:
The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its connection to the paper's core claims.

:modified_text:
The retraction-worthiness of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its connection to the paper's core claims.

:explanation:
This modification conflates two related but distinct concepts: the "criticality" of a scientific error and its "retraction-worthiness."

1.  **Criticality (Epistemic/Logical):** In the context of this proposal, criticality refers to the logical impact of an error on the paper's scientific conclusions. An error is critical if it invalidates a core claim. This is an intrinsic property of the error's relationship to the paper's argument. The methodology described (inserting "claim-invalidating errors") is designed to test this specific concept.

2.  **Retraction-worthiness (Procedural/Ethical):** This refers to whether an error warrants the formal, public withdrawal of the paper from the scientific literature. While a critical error can be grounds for retraction, the two are not equivalent. Retraction decisions involve a host of factors beyond pure logical criticality, including author intent (honest error vs. misconduct like data fabrication), the scope of the error's impact, and journal policies. For instance, a paper might be retracted for plagiarism even if its scientific claims are sound, while a paper with a genuine, claim-invalidating error might receive a correction (erratum) instead of a full retraction if the error was honest and limited in scope.

The modified hypothesis makes the flawed assumption that the epistemic concept of criticality is interchangeable with the procedural outcome of retraction-worthiness. The experiment, as designed, only tests for the former. It can determine if an LLM finds an error that breaks a claim, but it provides no evidence about whether that error would or should lead to a retraction, a much more complex judgment. The formal reasoning of the plan remains intact, but it rests on the undermined premise that these two concepts are the same.
