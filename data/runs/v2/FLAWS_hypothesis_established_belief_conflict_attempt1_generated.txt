:error_type:
Conflict with Established Beliefs

:original_text:
The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its connection to the paper's core claims.

:modified_text:
The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is determined by its logical position within the paper's deductive structure; any error that invalidates a premise from which a core claim is derived is, by definition, a critical error.

:explanation:
The modification introduces a conflict with the Duhem-Quine thesis, a well-established concept in the philosophy of science.

(a) The Duhem-Quine thesis states that it is impossible to test a scientific hypothesis in isolation, because an empirical test always relies on a web of background and auxiliary assumptions (e.g., about the functioning of measurement instruments, the purity of samples, or the validity of underlying theories). When an observation contradicts a prediction, one can only conclude that *something* in that web is wrong, but logic alone cannot pinpoint whether the error lies in the core hypothesis or in one of the auxiliary assumptions.

(b) The modified hypothesis is internally consistent but presents an oversimplified, purely deductive model of scientific validation. It asserts that an error's criticality is an absolute property determined *by definition* from its logical position within the paper alone. This directly clashes with the Duhem-Quine thesis, which implies that the "criticality" of an error is not absolute. An error that logically invalidates a premise could, in practice, be accommodated by the scientific community by adjusting an auxiliary assumption, thereby saving the core claim. The modified hypothesis incorrectly treats the paper as a closed, self-contained logical system, ignoring the complex web of external assumptions that an expert would know are always in play.
