## Problem

Peer review is the cornerstone of scientific validation, but it is strained by the exponential growth of research output, particularly in fields like AI and Machine Learning. The limited pool of expert reviewers struggles to keep pace, leading to an increased risk of significant errors being overlooked in published work. These errors can misdirect future research and carry substantial costs. While Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise in assisting with review tasks, their core ability to identify and localize substantive errors that invalidate a paper's claims remains largely unevaluated.

Existing benchmarks for this task are insufficient. Those based on real-world retracted papers suffer from noisy labels, as the stated reasons for retraction are often vague and may not be the only flaws present. Conversely, benchmarks using synthetic errors are often limited to specific error types or domains and tend to evaluate error detection indirectly, for instance, by measuring changes in a generated review score.

Our research is motivated by the need for a rigorous, scalable, and automated benchmark to directly assess an LLM's ability to find critical flaws in scientific papers. We hypothesize that by grounding synthetic errors in a paper's core claims, rather than using a fixed set of error types, we can create a more relevant and challenging testbed. Our primary research question is: How effectively can state-of-the-art LLMs identify and localize claim-invalidating errors in scientific papers when explicitly prompted to do so?

## Hypothesis

The criticality of an error in a scientific paper is a function of its connection to the paper's core claims.

## Method

We will develop an automated pipeline to construct a benchmark, which we will name FLAWS (Fault Localization Across Writing in Science), and an accompanying evaluation framework.

**1. Benchmark Construction:**

Our approach will center on systematically inserting controlled, claim-invalidating errors into high-quality scientific papers.

*   **Paper Selection:** We will begin by selecting a corpus of recent, peer-reviewed papers from a top-tier conference. This ensures a baseline of quality and minimizes the risk of the papers being present in the training data of the LLMs we intend to evaluate. We will obtain the LaTeX source for these papers.
*   **Claim-Invalidating Error Insertion:** To ensure errors are meaningful and relevant, we will use an LLM-driven, multi-step process:
    1.  **Claim Extraction:** We will first prompt an LLM to extract the main, falsifiable scientific claims from each paper.
    2.  **Error Generation:** For each claim, we will instruct an LLM to modify one or more segments of the paper's text to introduce a sophisticated, domain-appropriate conceptual error that undermines that specific claim.
    3.  **Multi-Step Filtration:** To guarantee the quality and difficulty of the errors, we will apply a rigorous filtering process. This includes using an LLM to discard invalid modifications (e.g., those that merely surface an existing limitation) and a two-step process to filter out errors that are too easy. The second step, which we term "internal identification," involves tasking the error-generating LLM with finding the error it just created; if it succeeds, the error is discarded as being too simple.
*   **Ground Truth Localization:** For each successfully inserted error, we will prompt an LLM to identify all text excerpts in the paper that are rendered incorrect by the modification. This complete set of excerpts will serve as the ground truth for our evaluation.

**2. Automated Evaluation Framework:**

We will design an automated framework to assess an LLM's performance on the error identification task.

*   **Task Definition:** The evaluation task will require a model to identify and extract erroneous text excerpts from a compiled PDF of a modified paper. Models will be asked to return a ranked list of up to 10 potential error excerpts.
*   **Evaluation Metric:** To determine if a model has successfully identified an error, we will use a dual-metric approach. An error will be considered found if it is flagged by either of the following:
    1.  **Word-Level Levenshtein Similarity:** We will compute a normalized, word-level Levenshtein similarity score between the model-generated excerpts and the ground-truth error excerpts. This metric is robust to minor formatting differences while preserving semantic order.
    2.  **LLM-as-a-Judge:** We will prompt an LLM to act as a judge, deciding whether a model's identified excerpt corresponds to the ground-truth error.

## Experiment Design

**1. Corpus and Benchmark Generation:**

*   We will source our papers from the accepted list of the 2025 International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), ensuring they were first posted on arXiv after the knowledge cutoff dates of the models we plan to test.
*   To create the FLAWS benchmark, we will use two distinct, state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT 5) for the error insertion process. This will mitigate model-specific biases in error generation.
*   We will apply our full pipeline of claim extraction, error generation, and multi-step filtering. The final paper-error pairs that survive this process will be compiled into PDFs, forming the benchmark dataset.

**2. Model Evaluation:**

*   We will evaluate a set of five frontier LLMs on the FLAWS benchmark.
*   For each paper-error pair, we will provide the PDF to each LLM and prompt it to identify and return up to 10 ranked excerpts containing potential errors. We will impose constraints on the maximum number and length of excerpts to prevent trivial solutions.
*   We will use our automated evaluation framework to score each model's output against the ground truth. The primary performance metric will be Accuracy@k, representing the fraction of papers where the error was correctly identified within the top-k returned excerpts.

**3. Validation of Methodology:**

*   To validate the reliability of our automated pipeline, we will conduct a human annotation study on a randomly selected subset of the benchmark.
*   Two human annotators will assess (1) the plausibility and topical relevance of the inserted scientific errors and (2) the correctness of the LLMs' identifications.
*   We will measure inter-annotator agreement using Krippendorff's alpha. We will then compare the results of our automated evaluation metric against the human consensus to confirm its alignment with human judgment.
